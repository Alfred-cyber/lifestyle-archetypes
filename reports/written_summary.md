## What we did

Many children and teenagers spend a large amount of time online for school, socialising and entertainment. While internet use has many benefits, excessive or uncontrolled use has been linked to poorer sleep, reduced physical activity and mental health difficulties. However, researchers still do not fully understand how these lifestyle factors combine in everyday life.

In this study, we used information from the Child Mind Institute Healthy Brain Network to investigate whether children naturally grouped into different lifestyle patterns based on their sleep, physical activity and body composition. Rather than looking at each factor separately, we used machine learning to identify groups of children with similar overall lifestyles. We then explored whether these groups differed in problematic internet use or mental health measures.


## What we found

Our analysis identified two statistical clusters, but these did not represent clear or clinically meaningful lifestyle archetypes.

The first cluster contained most participants and showed sleep patterns that were generally consistent with what would be expected for children and adolescents.

The second cluster appeared to have extremely short estimated sleep durations—often less than two hours per night. Because this is not biologically realistic for most healthy children, we believe this cluster was most likely caused by limitations in how sleep was estimated from the wearable devices rather than representing a genuine lifestyle pattern.

When we compared these two groups, we found no meaningful differences in problematic internet use, depression symptoms or reported internet use. The only significant difference was in a clinician-rated measure of overall functioning, but this result alone was not enough to conclude that the clusters represented meaningful lifestyle groups.

We also explored an alternative analysis with three clusters. This exploratory analysis suggested that problematic internet use tended to increase across the three groups. Although this finding is interesting, it was not part of our primary validated analysis and therefore should be interpreted cautiously until confirmed by future studies.


## How these patterns relate to problematic internet use and mental health

Overall, our study did not find strong evidence that children naturally fall into distinct lifestyle groups that are associated with problematic internet use.

This does not mean that sleep, physical activity or body composition are unimportant. Previous research has shown that these factors are related to health and wellbeing. Instead, our findings suggest that these relationships may be more gradual and continuous rather than forming a small number of clearly defined lifestyle types.

Our exploratory analysis hints that more subtle behavioural patterns may exist, but further research is needed before firm conclusions can be drawn.

Importantly, these findings describe trends across a large group of children and should not be used to make decisions about any individual child.


## What this might mean

This study highlights both the potential and the challenges of using machine learning to better understand children's health behaviours.

Future research could improve on this work by using more accurate methods to estimate sleep from wearable devices, including additional lifestyle factors such as diet, screen time, family environment and socioeconomic circumstances, and testing the findings in larger and more diverse populations.

Understanding how multiple lifestyle behaviours interact may eventually help researchers develop more personalised approaches to supporting healthy internet use and mental wellbeing in children and adolescents.


## Important caveats

- The identified clusters are exploratory statistical groupings, not medical or psychological diagnoses.
- The main validated analysis did not identify clinically meaningful lifestyle archetypes associated with problematic internet use.
- The three-cluster findings are exploratory and require confirmation in independent datasets.
- The study included only participants with sufficiently complete wearable data, so the findings may not represent all children.
- No child should be labelled, assessed or treated based on which statistical cluster they most closely resemble.
