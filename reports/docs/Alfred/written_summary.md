# Lifestyle Archetypes and Problematic Internet Use — Summary

*A summary for a non-specialist audience.*

## What we did

We looked at data from nearly 4,000 children and teenagers to see whether the way they sleep,
move, and are built (their body composition) sorts them into different "lifestyle types," and
whether those types are connected to unhealthy internet habits or mental health. A wrist-worn
activity tracker gave us objective information on sleep and movement, which we combined with
body-composition measurements — deliberately leaving out age, sex, and any internet-use or
mental-health scores, so the computer grouped children on lifestyle alone, "blind" to the
question we ultimately wanted to ask.

## What we found

This project's honest headline finding is that **our main analysis did not turn up a real
lifestyle type linked to problematic internet use** — and figuring out *why* it didn't is itself
a useful result. Here is what happened, step by step:

- **The clearest, most statistically solid grouping split children into two groups.** By every
  standard measure, this was an excellent split — it separated children cleanly, and it barely
  changed even when we reran it on different random subsets of the data or added a little noise.
  On paper, this looked like a strong, dependable finding.

- **But when we looked at *why* the two groups were different, it wasn't a real lifestyle
  contrast.** Almost the entire split came down to one number: estimated sleep length. One group
  averaged a normal, healthy-sounding 7.9 hours of sleep a night. The other group averaged **under
  2 hours a night** — which isn't a realistic amount of sleep for a healthy child to sustain. This
  tells us that, for this second group, the tracker most likely failed to measure their sleep
  properly (for example, because the device was taken off overnight, or their sleep pattern didn't
  match our simple "asleep between 10pm and 8am" rule) — not that these children genuinely barely
  sleep. On every other measure we looked at — body mass, body fat, muscle mass, self-reported
  activity level — the two groups were statistically indistinguishable.

- **This two-group split showed no meaningful link to problematic internet use or mental
  health.** Internet-use scores, screen-time hours, and a sleep-disturbance questionnaire were all
  statistically similar between the two groups. One clinician-rated wellbeing score did differ,
  but since nothing else lined up with it, we don't treat that single result as strong evidence on
  its own.

- **A follow-up, more exploratory check hinted at something more promising.** When we asked the
  computer to find three groups instead of two, using a different set of settings, a clearer
  pattern emerged: average internet-use scores rose in steps across the three groups (roughly 24,
  then 27, then 33 points on the scale used) — a difference unlikely to be due to chance. This is
  only from a single alternative run, so we're treating it as a promising lead worth chasing, not
  as a confirmed finding.

In short: rather than three to five confidently-named lifestyle archetypes, what we have is a
**well-supported null result** for the cleanest split we could find, plus **one promising but
unconfirmed lead** from a follow-up check, pointing toward where a better-designed future study
should look.

## How these patterns relate to problematic internet use and mental health

The main, trustworthy finding here is a "no" — the most reliable lifestyle split we found was not
connected to problematic internet use, sleep disturbance, or self-reported screen time in this
data. That's a meaningful result: it tells us that a simple two-way split on this particular set
of measurements isn't the right lens for finding this relationship, at least with the wearable-
based sleep estimate we used.

The three-group exploratory follow-up suggests a relationship *might* exist — with internet-use
scores increasing across the three groups in a way that looked statistically real — but this
needs to be treated cautiously. It came from one alternative analysis, using different settings
than our main, carefully-validated approach, and hasn't yet been put through the same
stability checks. **These are preliminary, population-level associations from a subset of
participants, not predictions or diagnoses for any individual child**, and the same caution
applies even more strongly to the three-group lead than to the main analysis.

## What this might mean

- The biggest actionable takeaway from this project isn't a lifestyle type — it's a **measurement
  lesson**: simple, movement-based sleep estimates from wearables can fail quietly for a
  meaningful share of children, and that failure can masquerade as a strong, statistically clean
  "finding" if you don't look closely at what's actually driving it. Any future work using this
  kind of data should check for this before trusting a clean-looking cluster.
- The promising three-group, step-wise pattern in internet-use scores is worth chasing further,
  ideally with a better sleep-measurement approach and a dedicated stability check, before
  treating it as a real effect.
- Because only about 1 in 5 children in the full study had usable tracker data, and device-wearing
  itself may not be random (some children may be more or less likely to wear the device
  consistently), any future study should also think carefully about who is — and isn't —
  represented in the tracker data.

## Important caveats

- These are exploratory, population-level patterns, not clinical categories, and the main
  two-group split described above turned out to reflect a data-quality issue rather than a real
  lifestyle difference.
- Associations with problematic internet use and mental health scores are preliminary and based
  on a subset of participants (about 1 in 5 of the full sample) who had usable wearable-tracker
  data.
- The three-group, step-wise internet-use pattern is a single, exploratory result that has not
  yet been independently confirmed or stability-tested, and should not be treated as established.
- No child should be labelled or assessed based on which group they resemble in this analysis —
  the internet-use and mental-health measures used here (PCIAT, CGAS, sleep-disturbance scores)
  are screening tools, not diagnoses.
